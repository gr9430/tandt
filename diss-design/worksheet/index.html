Critical making: method reference

Open any section for the details. Everything here also prints.

What is critical making?

Critical making is a research method that uses material, hands-on work with technologies to extend critical reflection. Matt Ratto defines it as the effort “to use material forms of engagement with technologies to supplement and extend critical reflection and, in doing so, to reconnect our lived experiences with technologies to social and conceptual critique” (Ratto 253).

The things made are not meant to be finished products. Prototypes are “used to extend knowledge and skills in relevant technical areas as well as to provide the means for conceptual exploration” (Ratto 253). The knowledge comes through the making.

In what fields is critical making used?
  • Science and technology studies and information studies (Ratto)
  • Technology and society; engineering pedagogy (Mann)
  • Digital humanities (Smithies)
  • DH methodologies and pedagogy (Johnson & Salter)
How it works
  1. Gather the concepts. Review the relevant literature and name the concepts and questions the making will work through.
  2. Build prototypes together. Design and build technical prototypes collaboratively. Function matters less than what the building teaches.
  3. Reconfigure and reflect. Rework the prototypes, discuss what they show, and carry that back to the concepts.

Paraphrased from Ratto (2011)

Strengths and Limitations
StrengthsLimitations
  • Understanding comes through “joint conceptual and materially productive work” (Ratto 258)
  • Connects lived experience with technology to critique
  • Makes abstract claims about technology concrete and testable
  • Produces a shared object a group can discuss
  • The mapping of theory onto technology can turn reductive (Ratto 257)
  • “Not a model for general use”: upkeep, one maker's skills, platform dependence (Smithies)
  • The purpose can look opaque to colleagues (Smithies)
  • AI is often handled expediently: one optional prompt, not a full module (Johnson & Salter)
Key considerations for researchers
  • Positionality: who counts as an insider, and when does “insider” become a credential check? (Mann)
  • Shared authority: at Kamĩrĩĩthũ, the community made from its own archive and the playwrights' voice was one among many (Ngũgĩ).
  • Visible steps: document the process. The less of a process you can see into, the more you owe an account of what it did.
  • Maintenance: plan for who keeps the thing running after the study ends.
  • Platform dependence: even a self-hosted stack can still run on Amazon (Smithies).
How critical making compares to other making methods
MethodCentral questionHow it differs from critical making
Research through designWhat can designing this artifact teach us?Aims at design knowledge and better artifacts; critique of technology is not required
Speculative designWhat futures could this object provoke us to imagine?Objects provoke an audience, often as exhibits; making is less a site of the maker's own learning
Participatory designHow can the people affected shape the system?Centers stakeholder decision-making in a system's design; reflection is a means rather than the end
Maker / DIY cultureCan I build or fix this myself?Making as skill, hobby, or self-reliance; critique is optional (Mann's “do it together” pushes it toward critique)
How I plan to use critical making in my dissertation

Research question: How can critical making, practiced as distant making, build digital literacy by teaching tactile literacy through digital tools, and digital literacy through tactile making, when people work with large data sets?

I am integrating my critical making practice into digital literacy by developing distant making: an insider steps back from an archive they know closely and makes something new from the whole, using processes they can see into. “My distance is a matter of choice” (Ritchey, “What Ziner is Talking About”).

  • Ziner is the prototype: a zine tool for first-year composition and the Ephemera Eternal workshop, whose iterations stay visible in its commit history.
  • Recoding workshops move between the folded page and the tools that generate it. Participants make from their own cultural data sets, and follow-up interviews ask what the making decoded (Hall).
Example studies using critical making

Ratto (2011): flwr.

Ratto, Matt. “Critical Making: Conceptual and Material Studies in Technology and Social Life.” The Information Society, vol. 27, no. 4, July 2011, pp. 252–60, https://doi.org/10.1080/01972243.2011.583819.

Participants built networked “flwrs” set to a gift economy. When most of them only listened, the network “died,” and the discussion turned to reciprocity.

Mann (2014): maktivism.

Mann, Steve. “Maktivism: Authentic Making for Technology in the Service of Humanity.” DIY Citizenship: Critical Making and Social Media, edited by Matt Ratto and Megan Boler, The MIT Press, 2014, https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9568.003.0004.

Students hack dumpster computers and ID readers. The “Griefcase” makes a guard who wants to search it give up his own fingerprints.

Smithies (2017): Full Stack DH.

Smithies, James. “Full Stack DH: Building a Virtual Research Environment on a Raspberry Pi.” Making Things and Drawing Boundaries: Experiments in the Digital Humanities, edited by Jentery Sayers, University of Minnesota Press, 2017, pp. 102–14, https://doi.org/10.5749/j.ctt1pwt6wq.13.

A personal research environment on a Raspberry Pi: control of the whole stack, yet still dependent on Amazon, and still needing care.

Johnson & Salter (2025): “Generation.”

Johnson, Emily, and Anastasia Salter. Critical Making in the Age of AI. Amherst College Press, 2025.

Students regenerate their own assignments with ChatGPT and critique the output.

Spark Notes

  1. A preliminary history: Kamĩrĩĩthũ. A community theatre shaped its play collectively, making from its own cultural archive; the playwrights' authority was one voice among many (Ngũgĩ, Decolonising the Mind).
  2. What critical making is. Material engagement with technologies that supplements and extends critical reflection; prototypes built for conceptual exploration, not function (Ratto 253).
  3. Where it's used, and what to watch for. STS, engineering pedagogy, and digital humanities. Its strength is understanding through making. Its risks: a reductive mapping of theory onto technology, upkeep that depends on one maker, a purpose that looks opaque to colleagues, and AI handled expediently.
  4. Four studies that made things. Ratto's flwr network, Mann's maktivism, Smithies's Full Stack DH, and Johnson & Salter's “Generation” assignment.
  5. Tensions.
    • Authentic maker vs. “poseur”: when does “insider” become a credential check?
    • A lone scholar's stack vs. doing it together
    • Control vs. dependence
    • Platform vs. local
  6. (Un)making. Agentic tools can become another walled garden or build alternatives to one. Unmake the agent (reject, supplement, extend, critique), and treat maintenance as resistance (Johnson & Salter).
  7. Distant making, from practice. Ziner as prototype → distant making (an insider steps back from an archive they know closely and makes something new from the whole, using processes they can see into) → recoding workshops, where participants make from their own cultural data.
